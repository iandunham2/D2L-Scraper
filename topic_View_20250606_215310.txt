After reviewing Marc Maron's reading this week, the one example of a person whose identity is visible through the narrative was Barack Obama. He talks about being raised by a single mother as an African American in a place that is not grounded in a lot of African-American culture. He goes further to speak on how he struggled with his identity based on how he is viewed and seen as a black man in America. He started absorbing and indulging in a lot of stereotypes just to fit in until he became a young adult and realized that that was not him. Every persona he took on (being a rebel, tough guy, etc.) was because he was insecure and now he was facing the reality of who he really is and what he believes in. I chose that example because while I do not relate to his situation, I know a lot of bi-racial Americans do.
While scrolling for a familiar name in the list of episodes of Marc Maron's WTF Podcast, I found Lupita Nyong'o. She is interviewed in episode 1572. I was interested in her because I liked her role in Disney's Black Panther. She seemed very interesting and charismatic from that movie alone. After listening to this episode, I found out some interesting things. Lupita talked about how she was born in Mexico and grew up in Kenya. Her dad was a politician who pushed for democracy in Kenya due to its autocracy. The autocratic rule oppressed her aspiration to pursue acting because creative expression was suppressed in her country. Mind you, she was only 1 when she moved to Kenya. She also dealt with colorism and colonialism. All of that would have a major effect on one's identity. In Kenya, she spoke on how they adhered to "European beauty standards". The lighter you are the more you are valued", she said. During her time growing up in Mexico, she experienced a weird yet celebrity-type experience. It was weird because she felt objectified.
The colorism and colonialism Lupita experienced influenced her view of the world, which led to her making deliberate choices in her career as an actor. She chose to act in certain films that defy Hollywood's typecasting.
